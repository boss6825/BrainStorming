# 03 · Combinatorial Creativity

> *"An idea is nothing more nor less than a new combination of old elements."* — James Webb Young

The premise: **nothing is wholly original — new ideas are new combinations of existing parts.** So the way to manufacture novelty on demand is to force collisions between things that don't normally meet. The more diverse your building blocks, the more interesting the result.

This file has two layers: **four framings** (the "why" — good as system prompts / mindset primers) and **eight methods** (the "how" — tactical generators). They compose: pick a framing to set the mind, then run a method.

## Framing: Combinatorial Creativity (Popova)

The umbrella idea. Creativity is combinatorial — we create by connecting existing bits of knowledge into new configurations. Use this as an opening system prompt for any session.

```
You are a combinatorial idea generator. Nothing is truly original — great ideas
are new combinations of existing pieces.
Domain/problem: {problem_or_goal}
Step 1: List 15 diverse "building blocks" relevant (even loosely) to this domain —
concepts, tools, mental models, historical examples, and ideas borrowed from
unrelated fields ({biology, music, logistics, games}).
Step 2: Cross-pollinate. Produce 8 new ideas, each explicitly formed by combining
2-3 of the blocks above. For each, name the blocks used and describe the idea in
2 sentences.
Step 3: Flag the 2 combinations that are least obvious yet most promising.
```

### A. Conceptual Blending (Fauconnier & Turner)

New meaning emerges from an *integration network*: two input spaces, a generic space (the structure they share), and a blended space where selectively-projected elements combine to create *emergent* structure present in neither input.

```
Apply Fauconnier & Turner conceptual blending to fuse two domains.
Input Space A: {domain_A}
Input Space B: {domain_B}
1. Generic space: identify the abstract structure/roles A and B share (actors,
   resources, flows, goals).
2. Cross-map: match counterpart elements between A and B via that shared structure.
3. Blend: selectively project elements from BOTH into a new blended space. Describe
   the resulting concept and any EMERGENT properties neither A nor B had alone.
4. Produce 3 distinct blends; give each a name and a one-line pitch.
Target application: {application_or_product}
```

### B. Bisociation (Koestler)

From *The Act of Creation*: insight happens when you perceive a situation in two self-consistent but habitually *incompatible* frames at once — the collision of two "matrices" produces the spark.

```
Use Koestler's bisociation: force a collision between two habitually incompatible
frames of reference.
Frame 1: {problem_or_subject}
Frame 2: {unrelated_frame, e.g. "a jazz improvisation", "an ant colony", "a
courtroom trial"}
1. Describe the internal logic and "rules" of each frame separately.
2. Find the point where the two intersect — where the same element belongs to both.
3. Generate 5 ideas that live at that intersection. For each, explain which rule
   from Frame 2 is being imported into Frame 1 to create the surprise.
```

### C. Adjacent Possible & Liquid Networks (Johnson)

From *Where Good Ideas Come From*: innovation happens at the "adjacent possible" — the set of next-step recombinations reachable from what already exists. Don't leap too far ahead; recombine what's on the table.

```
Explore the "adjacent possible" for: {current_product_or_idea}
1. Map the components/capabilities that already exist ("what's on the table").
2. Generate the adjacent possible: 10 ideas reachable by ONE new recombination of
   existing components — nothing requiring a leap too far ahead of today.
3. Rate each on how "adjacent" vs "ahead of its time" it is (1-5).
4. Now simulate a liquid network: introduce ideas/components from these adjacent
   fields — {adjacent_fields} — and generate 5 more recombinations at the new frontier.
```

### D. A Technique for Producing Ideas (James Webb Young)

The classic 5-step process. Especially good because steps 3–4 (incubate, then let the idea return) map onto how associative minds actually work.

```
Run James Webb Young's 5-step idea process on: {problem}
Step 1 (Gather): List the SPECIFIC material (facts about {problem/product/audience})
   and the GENERAL material (unrelated principles, stories, patterns worth combining).
Step 2 (Digest): Combine the facts in different pairings; note relationships and
   partial fits between specific and general material.
Step 3 (Incubate): List the tangential/associative leaps a rested mind might make.
Step 4 (Eureka): Produce the resulting new combinations of old elements — 7 ideas.
Step 5 (Shape): Take the best idea and adapt it to real constraints ({constraints});
   list its weaknesses and fixes.
```

## Methods

### 1. Forced Connections / Random Word

Deliberately pair the problem with a random unrelated stimulus, extract *its* attributes, and force-fit them onto the problem. (Same engine as [01 §4](01-creativity-frameworks.md#4-random-word--random-stimulus) and [02 §2](02-neurodivergent-thinking-modes.md#2-random-provocation) — kept here as the canonical combinatorial version.)

```
Use the Forced Connections / random-word technique.
Challenge: {problem_or_goal}
Random stimulus: {random_word}  (if blank, pick a vivid, unrelated concrete noun)
1. List 8 attributes, associations, and behaviors of "{random_word}" — ignore my
   problem for now.
2. For each attribute, force a connection: how could it suggest a solution, feature,
   or angle for the challenge?
3. Output the 6 strongest forced-fit ideas, each as: [attribute] --> [idea].
Repeat with a second random word if I say "again".
```

### 2. Morphological Matrix

Zwicky's method: independent parameters × options → a combinatorial space you screen for coherent novelty. (Full template in [01 §8](01-creativity-frameworks.md#8-morphological-analysis-zwicky-box).) Best when the value is genuinely in *which combination* you pick.

### 3. Analogical / Cross-Industry Transfer

Transfer a solved solution *principle* from a distant domain. (Full template in [01 §10](01-creativity-frameworks.md#10-analogical--cross-industry-transfer); structural variant in [02 §4](02-neurodivergent-thinking-modes.md#4-cross-domain-pattern-transfer).) The research finding worth remembering: distant analogies that share *relational* structure (not surface features) produce the most paradigm-shifting ideas.

### 4. Mashup / "X for Y"

Transplant a proven model/mechanic from domain X into market Y ("Spotify for X", "Uber for Y"). Fast to generate and to communicate — but only good when it leads with real customer value, not just the analogy.

```
Generate mashup concepts using the "X for Y" pattern.
Anchor: {domain_or_model_X, e.g. "Spotify's playlist/recommendation model"}
Target market/context: {market_Y}
1. Extract the 4 core mechanics that make X work (business model, UX pattern, value driver).
2. Transplant each into {market_Y}. Produce 6 "X for Y" concepts.
3. Write each as: "We are [X] for [Y] that does [Z impact]."
4. Stress-test the top 2: does the target customer actually want it this way, and
   do the unit economics hold? Name the key risk for each.
```

### 5. Synectics

Gordon's method uses four analogy mechanisms to defamiliarize a problem — make the familiar strange and the strange familiar.

```
Apply Synectics to make the familiar strange for: {problem}
Run all four analogy mechanisms and pull one idea from each:
- Direct: how is this solved in nature or an unrelated field? {optional hint}
- Personal: "become" the core object/process — describe the problem from its
  first-person point of view, then say what it "wants".
- Symbolic: compress the essence into a paradoxical two-word phrase (a "compressed
  conflict", e.g. "reliable chaos"); derive an idea from the tension.
- Fantasy: if any law of physics/economics could be broken, what's the ideal magical
  solution? Then find the closest real-world approximation.
Summarize 4 ideas, one per mechanism.
```

### 6. Biomimicry

Innovate by emulating strategies life has already evolved. The core move is *function-first*: abstract what the design must **do**, then ask how nature does it.

```
Use biomimicry (function-first) on: {design_challenge}
1. Reframe the challenge as a function question: "How does nature {verb the
   function — 'cool without energy', 'stick reversibly', 'distribute without a
   center'}?"
2. List 6 organisms, ecosystems, or natural processes that perform this function well.
3. For each, describe the biological STRATEGY (the mechanism, not the jargon).
4. Translate each into a concrete design idea for my challenge.
5. Note which strategies also improve sustainability/resource use.
```

### 7. Systematic Inventive Thinking (SIT)

Boyd & Goldenberg's "inside the box" method: apply five recombination patterns to a *closed world* of existing components. Counterintuitively, working within the existing parts (not adding new ones) produces the most inventive results.

```
Apply the 5 SIT patterns to: {product_or_service}
First list its components and immediate environment (the "closed world"). Then:
- SUBTRACTION: remove an essential component. What new use/benefit emerges?
- MULTIPLICATION: copy a component but change the copy somehow. What results?
- DIVISION: split a component (physically or functionally) and rearrange it in
  time or space.
- TASK UNIFICATION: assign an additional job to an existing component/resource.
- ATTRIBUTE DEPENDENCY: make two previously independent attributes vary together
  ("as X changes, Y automatically changes").
Give 2 ideas per pattern (10 total), then pick the 3 most inventive-yet-feasible.
```

### 8. Convergence Wrapper

Combinatorial methods over-generate — that's the point. Append this after *any* generator to converge honestly.

```
Now converge: score each idea 1-5 on novelty, feasibility, and fit to
{goal/constraints}. Show a table, then recommend the top 3 with a one-line
rationale and the single biggest risk for each.
```

**Compose freely.** Two high-yield combinations: (a) *Conceptual Blending → Convergence Wrapper* for fusing two domains into a named concept; (b) *Forced Connections → Adjacent Possible* to generate wild sparks and then pull them back to what's actually buildable now.
